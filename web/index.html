The idea

Ordinary encryption relies on hard maths. Quantum key distribution relies on physics: reading a quantum state in the wrong way changes it. If Alice sends a photon polarized at 0° or 90° and Bob measures it at 45°, the answer he gets is a coin flip, and the original information is gone.

BB84, published by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984, turns that disturbance into an alarm. Alice and Bob never trust the channel. They only trust the error rate they measure afterwards, and an eavesdropper cannot avoid raising it.

Four moves

1

Encode

Alice picks a random bit and a random basis, then sends one photon polarized to match.

2

Measure

Bob picks a basis at random. The right basis returns the bit. The wrong one returns a coin flip.

3

Sift

They announce their bases over a public line, never the bits, and keep only the matching positions.

4

Test

They compare a sample of the key. Too many mismatches means someone measured the photons.

Measuring a quantum state disturbs it. Eve cannot look without leaving a mark.

The simulator

Polarization symbols: basis + uses ─ for 0 and │ for 1. Basis × uses ╱ for 0 and ╲ for 1.

Send a message

BB84 does not carry your text through the photons. It creates a shared random key, and that key locks the message with XOR. The locked message then travels openly, and only someone with the same key can unlock it.

Bar width is the error rate (QBER), scaled so 50% fills the track. Above about 11%, Alice and Bob discard the key.

Why Eve gets caught

½ × ½ = ¼

Eve guesses the wrong basis half the time, and a wrong basis scrambles the photon.

When that happens, Bob's result is wrong half the time, because it is a coin flip.

Half of a half is a quarter, so intercepting every photon gives about 25% errors in the sifted key.
